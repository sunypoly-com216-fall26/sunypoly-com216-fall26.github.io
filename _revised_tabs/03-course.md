## COM 216: AI and the Human Future

> COM 216 is a history of human communication told through one question: when the technologies of communication change, what happens to reading, writing, memory, attention, and thought?

For most of human history, people generated meaning and tools helped carry it. A voice carried speech across a room. Memory carried stories across generations. Writing carried language outside the body. Print made copies at scale. Electronic media accelerated transmission. The internet connected people, documents, images, platforms, and archives in ways that previous centuries could not have imagined.

Generative AI changes the pattern. It is not only a way to store, transmit, organize, or amplify human language. It generates fluent language at scale and on demand. The language is made fresh each time, in response to context, through probabilistic generation. That makes it useful. It also makes it strange. It can help you read, write, compare, plan, and explore. It can also produce errors with confidence, smooth over difficulty, and make shallow work look more complete than it is.

This course is about that difference. We place generative AI inside the long history of communication technologies and ask what changes when machines no longer only carry human language but begin generating it.

### The shape of the semester

The course moves through three broad zones. They are not the only possible map of communication history, but they give us a useful way to see the break that generative AI creates.

**I. The oral world**

Before writing, knowledge lived in bodies, voices, habits, stories, rituals, songs, and trained memory. Language happened between people. To know something was not simply to possess information; it was to be able to remember, perform, repeat, adapt, and pass it on. We begin here because the oral world makes one thing clear: memory is not just storage. It is a human practice.

**First break: writing**

Writing moves language outside the body. A message can travel farther than a voice. An argument can outlive the person who made it. A record can be checked, copied, taught, hidden, recovered, or disputed. This changes what evidence is. It changes what authority is. It changes what it means to know something.

**II. Alphabet to social media**

Writing stabilizes language. Alphabets make writing easier to learn and reproduce. Print scales copying. Electronic media accelerate circulation. The internet connects archives, institutions, markets, and people. Social media and search platforms then organize attention: they do not usually write the language for us, but they shape what we see, what we miss, and what spreads.

In this zone, humans are still generating the language. Technologies store it, transmit it, sort it, rank it, remix it, and amplify it. That is already powerful enough to reshape politics, identity, memory, education, and daily life.

**Second break: the transformer**

The transformer marks the break this course is built around. Generative AI does not only move language around. It produces language that can function in a conversation, draft, summary, explanation, plan, or argument. Whether that language is good, accurate, ethical, useful, or appropriate is another question. Learning to ask that question well is part of the work.

**III. The transformer era**

The course spends the most time here because this is where you are entering college. You will use generative AI to study generative AI, which means the object of study is also one of the tools of study. That is not a trick. It is the condition of the course.

We will ask practical and historical questions together. What infrastructures make these systems possible? Who pays for them? What data, labor, energy, institutions, and platforms sit behind them? What happens to privacy, identity, security, work, and well-being when generative AI becomes part of ordinary digital life? What happens to reading and writing when students can talk with a machine that produces fluent language on demand?

The course does not ask you to fear generative AI or celebrate it. It asks you to understand it precisely enough to use it well, question it carefully, and recognize what kinds of human judgment are now required.

### What you do

You read across a long history, from oral culture and writing to contemporary generative AI. You use generative AI to help move through difficult material, but not to replace the work of reading. You discuss, test, compare, and revise. You write at every stage, from early questions to more finished arguments.

Most importantly, you learn to make your thinking visible. When you work with generative AI, the conversation itself matters. Your prompts, corrections, doubts, and revisions show how you are using the tool and where your judgment enters. In COM 216, that record becomes part of how we understand the work.
